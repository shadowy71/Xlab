    Experiment No. 2 · Introduction

    Before you begin

    Aim

    To determine the wavelengths of the different colours of light emitted by a mercury vapour lamp, using a plane diffraction grating and a spectrometer, and to compare the measured values with the standard wavelengths.

    Apparatus

    Spectrometer with collimator and telescope, plane diffraction grating (15 000 lines per inch), mercury vapour lamp, reading lens.

    Basic theory

    Parallel light from the collimator falls on the grating. In a direction θ the rays from adjacent slits differ in path by d sin θ, so a bright principal maximum appears wherever d sin θ = n λ.

    d sin θ = n λ

    d = 2.54 / 15 000 cm
    = 1.6933 × 10−4 cm ≈ 16 933 Å

    Measure each line on both sides of the central maximum. The half-separation gives θ, and λ = d sin θ / n.

    Diffraction

    Diffraction is the bending and spreading of a wave around the edges of an obstacle or through a narrow aperture. It becomes noticeable when the aperture is comparable with the wavelength, which is why a narrow slit produces a spreading beam of light.

    Diffraction grating and grating element

    A plane diffraction grating is a plate ruled with a very large number of equidistant, parallel slits of equal width. Adjacent slits are separated by the grating element d. For the grating in the manual, 15 000 lines are ruled in one inch:

    d = 2.54 / 15 000 cm = 1.693333 × 10−4 cm = 1.693333 × 10−6 m ≈ 16 933.33 Å

    Derivation of d sin θ = n λ

    Constructive interference and principal maxima

    Parallel rays from the collimator strike every slit and are diffracted in all directions. In a direction θ, the rays from adjacent slits have a path difference d sin θ. They reinforce each other when that path difference is a whole number of wavelengths, producing a principal maximum:

    d sin θ = n λ → sin θ = n λ / d → θ = sin−1(n λ / d)

    In every other direction the contributions cancel, which is why sharp, dark spaces appear between the bright lines.

    Why the colours appear at different angles

    The angle depends on the wavelength. For a fixed order and grating, sin θ is proportional to λ, so red light (λ ≈ 6390 Å) is diffracted through a larger angle than violet light (λ ≈ 4387 Å). This is the reverse of the order seen with a prism and is the basis of the measurement.

    Order of the spectrum

    The integer n is the order. The first-order spectrum (n = 1) is the brightest and is used in this experiment. A second-order spectrum appears at a larger angle whenever n λ / d ≤ 1. If n λ / d exceeds 1 the equation has no real solution, and no such order exists — the simulation rejects it instead of drawing an impossible line.

    Measuring the diffraction angle

    The telescope is set on the central (zero-order) image and then on the same spectral line on the left and on the right of it. Let the readings be θ1 and θ2; the angle between the two diffracted beams is

    2θ = |θ2 − θ1| (wrapped across 0°/360°) → θ = 2θ / 2

    Taking both sides cancels the zero error of the scale and any small error in setting the grating normal, so a raw vernier reading must never be substituted directly into the grating equation.

    Wavelength and percentage deviation

    With θ known and n = 1, the wavelength follows directly: λ = d sin θ. The manual's comparison formula is

    % deviation = ((λexp − λstd) / λstd) × 100

    A signed deviation shows whether the measurement is high or low; the absolute deviation gives the magnitude. Small misalignment, a wide slit, backlash in the tangent screw and the finite resolution of the scale all produce a few tenths of a percent, which is normal for this experiment.

    Rounding is applied only when values are displayed. Every calculation uses full-precision SI values.

      Model assumptions

      • The string is 1.00 m long in model space. Red and cyan dots are neighboring particles with fixed horizontal positions; each moves only vertically around equilibrium. The disturbance travels; the particles do not travel with it.
      • A point at distance x shows the source delayed by x/v, where v = 1.20 √(T / 1.50 N) m/s. Tension is in model units with linear density held constant; it is not a calibration of a real string.
      • Damping uses e−γ·travel time. Oscillate drives a sine source; Pulse sends one smooth half-cycle whose duration is set by frequency; Manual records the driver position.
      • Fixed end adds an inverted delayed copy, loose end adds an upright delayed copy, and no end adds no reflection. Reflections fade in near the prescribed left driver.
      • The animation uses fixed 1/120 s steps and clamps frame delays to 50 ms. Changing mode or end condition restarts at t = 0; other control changes apply immediately to the current model.
